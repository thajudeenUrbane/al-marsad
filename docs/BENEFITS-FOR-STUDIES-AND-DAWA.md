# Classroom and Conversation

**How one evidence graph serves Islamic Studies and Daʿwa — today, and at scale.**

Every claim below is labelled either **· BUILT** (true today, in the working demo) or **· THE VISION** (not built yet — architecturally supported, but waiting on a corpus that doesn't exist). Nothing here is oversold. See [the reviewer's brief](REVIEWERS-BRIEF.md) for the same discipline applied to the data model itself.

---

## One graph, two doors

A graduate student and a daʿwa volunteer at a campus stall are, more often than either realizes, asking the same question in different words: *"How do we actually know this?"*

The student asks it in a seminar — patient, footnoted, wanting the chain of transmission and the scholarly disagreement laid bare. The volunteer gets asked it standing up, in thirty seconds, by someone who just read a post claiming the Seerah is "just legend."

Most tools pick one of these two people. A textbook serves the student. A pamphlet serves the volunteer. Neither becomes the other on demand.

This system is built so **one underlying graph** — the same reports, chains, gradings, and honestly-marked gaps — opens as a footnoted page for the student, or a thirty-second answer for the volunteer. Both are drawing on the same evidence, only at a different depth.

## Part One — Islamic Studies

### 1. Seeing an isnād instead of reading about one — **· BUILT**

Most students meet ḥadīth criticism as a page of definitions: *muttaṣil*, *munqaṭiʿ*, *mursal*. The words stay abstract until someone has traced a real chain by hand — slow, and rarely done before graduate level.

In the working demo, a student opens one real report and watches the system check, narrator by narrator, whether each link could plausibly have met the next — dates, cities, all of it. All five links come back **plausible**. Then the field asking *"is this chain connected?"* is left empty, marked **awaiting a scholar**.

In one screen, a student sees exactly what *ʿilm al-rijāl* is for — and exactly where a machine's competence ends and a scholar's begins.

### 2. Comparing two centuries side by side — **· THE VISION**

A question like *"how did centres of scholarship shift between 700 CE and 1000 CE?"* currently means assembling the picture from a dozen books by hand. At scale, the same graph that stores one event filters to two years at once — scholars, students, institutions, geography — shown as two states of one evolving network, not two disconnected essays.

### 3. Tracing how a teaching travelled — **· THE VISION** (the data shape already exists)

"How did this narration reach Andalusia?" is answerable *in principle* from what a chain already is: simultaneously a record of transmission **and** of where each narrator lived. At scale, that becomes a traceable path across generations and geography — not a research project in itself.

**Also worth knowing:** because a grading is always attached to a named scholar rather than baked into the "fact," a comparative-religion or *uṣūl al-ḥadīth* course can put a Sunni evaluation and an academic-historical evaluation of the same report side by side, honestly, without the system quietly picking a winner.

## Part Two — Daʿwa

### 1. "How do you even know that happened?" — **· BUILT, in miniature**

Close to the single most common question a daʿwa volunteer hears about the Seerah. The honest answer is rarely a flat "yes, definitely" — it's a chain of transmission, a date of composition, and a named scholar's assessment.

The one worked example built so far shows exactly this shape: the earliest written copy on record of one particular report is dated roughly two and a half centuries after the event it describes. An entirely ordinary gap for oral-to-written transmission anywhere in the ancient world — and it's shown automatically, never hidden.

That, itself, is a daʿwa point worth making on its own terms: **Muslim scholars built an entire discipline to interrogate exactly this gap, by name, a thousand years before "source criticism" existed as a term in any other tradition.** A tool that shows its evidence instead of asserting its conclusion makes that argument visually, without a word of apologetics.

*(This example is illustrative and not yet scholar-verified — as noted on every screen it appears on.)*

### 2. A shield against fabricated quotes — **· THE VISION**

Fabricated ḥadīth and invented Seerah anecdotes circulate constantly, especially over social media. The architecture already distinguishes *"not yet in our records"* from *"no genuine source exists for this"* — two very different claims a searchable, sourced graph could one day surface automatically, turning "I don't think that's authentic" into "here is what actually exists, and here is what does not."

### 3. "How did Islam reach *our* region?" — **· THE VISION**

This exact question — *"how did Islam reach Kerala?"* — was one of the founding examples for this whole project. A regional daʿwa programme could eventually show its own community a real, sourced thread connecting them to the earliest generations: traders, routes, the teachers who followed. Turning "we are part of a very old story" from a claim into something a person can actually see.

**Also worth knowing:** a new Muslim or curious seeker can walk the Hijrah as an unfolding journey — people, place and tension arriving in sequence — rather than reading it as a list of facts. And an interfaith conversation can rest on a sourced document like the Constitution of Madinah rather than on assertion, because the relationships it describes — protection, treaty, alliance — are already first-class parts of the model.

## What's real today, and what's still ahead

| | Today | At scale |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence for one report, traced in full | ✅ built | — |
| A chain's contact-plausibility, checked automatically | ✅ built | — |
| Multiple traditions' gradings, kept separate and honest | ✅ modelled | needs more graded reports |
| Comparing two centuries, two regions, two scholars | — | needs the corpus to grow |
| A searchable shield against fabricated quotes | — | needs a large, sourced corpus |
| "How did Islam reach my region?", answered visually | — | needs regional data, not yet gathered |

Fifteen entities exist today. The claim of this document is not that the tool already does all of this — it's that **the architecture was built, from the first design decision, to be capable of all of it**, and that the parts already working are working honestly.
